## Replace existing: “My Water Heater Is Leaking: What Should I Do?”

# My Water Heater Is Leaking. What Should I Do?

Finding water around your water heater is never a great way to start the day.

The good news is that water around a water heater does not always mean the tank itself has failed. Sometimes the problem is a leaking connection, valve, or another repairable component.

But if the tank itself is leaking, the water heater generally needs to be replaced.

The first step is figuring out which situation you have.

## If there is an active leak, shut off the water first

If water is actively coming from the water heater, look for the cold-water shutoff valve on the pipe entering the top of the tank and turn it off.

That stops additional water from entering the heater and can greatly reduce the amount of water that ends up on the floor.

If you have an electric water heater and can safely access your electrical panel, turn off the water-heater breaker.

For a gas water heater, follow the shutoff instructions on the unit if necessary. If you smell gas, do not attempt to troubleshoot the water heater yourself. Leave the area and contact your gas utility.

If there is standing water around electrical equipment or you are not comfortable operating any of the shutoffs, don't take chances. Call for help instead.

## Where is the water actually coming from?

This is the question we usually want answered first.

Take a flashlight and look around the water heater without taking anything apart.

### Water near the top of the heater

Check the hot- and cold-water connections and the piping above the tank.

A leaking fitting or water connection can sometimes drip down the outside of the heater and make it look like the tank itself is leaking.

These problems are often repairable.

### Water coming from the T&P relief valve

The temperature and pressure relief valve, usually called the T&P valve, is a safety device attached to the water heater with a discharge pipe running from it.

Occasional discharge from this valve can indicate excessive pressure, excessive temperature, a problem with the valve itself, or another issue that should be investigated.

Don't plug or cap a leaking T&P valve. It is an important safety device.

### Water coming from the drain valve

Near the bottom of a tank water heater is a drain valve used for draining and flushing the tank.

If the water is clearly coming from this valve, the tank itself may still be fine. A leaking drain valve can often be repaired or replaced.

### Water coming from underneath the tank

This is the one we don't like to see.

If water is originating from the bottom of the actual tank, rather than dripping down from somewhere above, there is a good chance the internal tank has failed.

Once the steel tank has corroded through and begun leaking, there isn't a practical repair. The water heater needs to be replaced.

## Sometimes the source isn't obvious

Water has a way of traveling.

A small leak at the top of a water heater can run down behind the tank and appear at the bottom. Nearby plumbing can leak onto the heater. In some situations, condensation can also create moisture that looks like a leak.

So don't automatically assume that seeing water underneath the heater means you need a new one.

This is also why photos are so useful.

At Water Heater Boys, we can often get a pretty good idea of what is happening by looking at a few clear photos or a short video of the heater and talking with you by phone or text.

For a straightforward tank replacement, that can save you from having to schedule an appointment just to have someone come to the house and give you a price.

## Does the age of the water heater matter?

Definitely.

If you have a relatively new water heater and the problem is a leaking fitting or replaceable valve, repairing it may make perfect sense.

If you have an older tank that is beginning to leak from the tank itself, replacement is usually the answer.

The age also matters when deciding how much money it makes sense to put into repairing an older unit. A repair that makes sense on a 3-year-old heater may not make much sense on a heater already approaching the end of its useful life.

If you don't know the age, take a photo of the manufacturer's label on the side of the tank. The serial number can usually be used to determine when it was manufactured.

## Don't wait too long on a leaking tank

A tiny amount of water underneath an old water heater can be easy to ignore, especially if the heater is still producing hot water.

That's not a good reason to wait.

A tank that has started leaking because of internal corrosion is not going to repair itself. The opening can get larger, and the amount of water escaping can increase.

A leaking heater in a garage with a properly drained pan may be less immediately damaging than one sitting inside the house, upstairs, or next to finished flooring. Either way, once you know the tank itself is leaking, it is time to plan the replacement.

## What should I send Water Heater Boys?

If you're not sure what you're looking at, you don't have to diagnose it yourself.

Send us:

- A photo showing the entire water heater
- A photo of the area where you see water
- A photo of the connections and venting at the top
- A clear photo of the manufacturer's model and serial-number label
- Your city
- A short description of what happened and whether the water is still actively leaking

For many standard tank replacements, we can review that information and discuss the job with you by phone or text without requiring an initial visit to your home.

If the situation is more complicated or you're considering changing to tankless or a heat pump water heater, we'll let you know if we need to see the installation in person.

## Repair or replace? Start by finding the source

The most important distinction with a leaking water heater is simple:

**A leaking connection or replaceable component may be repairable. A leaking tank is not.**

If you're seeing water around your heater and aren't sure which one you have, send Water Heater Boys a few photos and we'll help you figure out the next step.

## Replace existing: “Signs Your Water Heater Is Failing”

# Signs Your Water Heater May Be Failing

Water heaters do not always give you much warning before they fail.

Sometimes there are obvious clues: water on the floor, no hot water, or a pilot that will not stay lit. Other times the signs are subtle enough that homeowners live with them for months without realizing the water heater may be nearing the end of its life.

The important thing is knowing which symptoms are worth paying attention to, especially if you have an older tank.

## 1. The water heater is starting to leak

This is the biggest warning sign.

That does not mean every drop of water around a water heater means the tank has failed. Water can leak from a fitting, valve, supply line, or another replaceable component.

But if water is actually coming from the tank itself, particularly from underneath the tank, replacement is usually the answer.

Once the internal steel tank has corroded through, there is no practical repair.

If you find water around your heater, try to determine where it is coming from and take a few photos. We can often tell quite a bit just by looking at them.

## 2. You're running out of hot water sooner than you used to

If the same household suddenly cannot get through the same number of showers without running out of hot water, something has changed.

With a tank water heater, sediment can accumulate over time and reduce the amount of usable hot water. There can also be problems with the burner, heating elements, thermostat, dip tube, or other components.

This does not automatically mean you need a new water heater.

But when reduced hot-water capacity starts happening on an older unit, it is worth looking at the age and overall condition of the heater before putting significant money into repairs.

## 3. The water heater is making noises it didn't make before

Water heaters make some normal operating sounds, so a little noise is not necessarily a problem.

What gets our attention is when an older tank suddenly starts making loud popping, rumbling, banging, or crackling sounds.

One common cause is sediment collecting at the bottom of the tank.

In the Bay Area, water conditions vary considerably depending on where you live, so some homes experience mineral buildup more quickly than others.

Noise by itself does not mean the water heater is about to fail tomorrow. But if the unit is older and you're hearing noises that were not there before, it is another piece of information to consider.

## 4. Your hot water is rusty or discolored

If you turn on the hot water and see brown, orange, or rusty-colored water, first compare it with the cold water.

If both the hot and cold water are discolored, the problem may be somewhere in the water supply or plumbing system rather than the water heater.

If the cold water is clear but the hot water is consistently rusty, corrosion inside the water heater becomes a possibility.

That deserves a closer look, particularly on an older tank.

## 5. You're having repeated problems getting hot water

A water heater that stops producing hot water does not necessarily need replacement.

Gas water heaters can have problems with igniters, thermocouples, gas control valves, burners, or other components. Electric heaters have their own replaceable parts such as heating elements and thermostats.

The bigger question is how old the heater is and whether you are dealing with an isolated repair or the beginning of repeated problems.

Putting money into a relatively new water heater can make perfect sense.

Putting money into one repair after another on an old tank often does not.

## 6. There is visible corrosion around the heater

Take a look at the fittings, connections, top of the tank, and areas around the water heater.

A little surface discoloration on a fitting does not necessarily mean the water heater is failing.

But significant corrosion, particularly when combined with moisture or an older tank, should not be ignored.

Corrosion can help point us toward where water has been leaking and whether the issue is with a connection or the water heater itself.

## 7. The water heater is getting old

Age matters, but there is no expiration date stamped on a water heater.

Traditional tank water heaters commonly last somewhere around 8 to 12 years, but some fail earlier and some continue operating well beyond that.

We regularly see homeowners who have no idea how old their water heater is.

If that's you, take a picture of the manufacturer's label. The serial number can usually be used to determine when the heater was made.

An older water heater that is working perfectly does not automatically need to be replaced.

But an older water heater combined with leaking, corrosion, reduced hot-water capacity, or repeated repairs is a different situation.

That's when it starts making sense to plan the replacement instead of waiting for the decision to be made for you.

## Should you replace a water heater before it fails?

Sometimes.

If your heater is in a garage with good drainage, you may be comfortable letting an older unit continue working while keeping an eye on it.

If that same aging water heater is upstairs, inside a finished part of the home, or somewhere a leak could cause significant damage, you may have a much lower tolerance for waiting until it fails.

There is no rule saying you need to replace a water heater just because it reaches a certain age.

We prefer looking at the whole situation: age, condition, location, repair history, and any warning signs the heater is giving you.

## Not sure how old your heater is or whether it needs replacement?

You don't necessarily need to schedule someone to come to your house just to start figuring it out.

For a standard tank water heater, send Water Heater Boys a few clear photos of:

- The entire water heater
- The connections and venting at the top
- Any leaking, rust, or corrosion you see
- The manufacturer's model and serial-number label

Also tell us your city and what the heater has been doing.

For many straightforward tank replacements, we can review the information and discuss your options and pricing by phone or text first.

If the situation requires an on-site evaluation, we'll tell you that too.

# Article #18
## Replace existing: “Tankless vs Traditional Water Heater”

# Tankless vs. Traditional Water Heater: Which Is Better for Your Home?

If your water heater needs to be replaced, one of the first decisions is whether to install another traditional tank or switch to tankless.

We install both, but in the Bay Area today, we do not automatically recommend converting to tankless.

Tankless water heaters have some real advantages. They take up less space, can provide hot water continuously, and can last a long time when properly installed and maintained.

But they also cost more to install, usually require more conversion work, and Bay Area regulations are expected to become much more restrictive for gas tankless systems beginning in 2031.

For many homeowners, replacing an existing gas tank with another gas tank is still the more practical choice.

## The biggest advantage of tankless: you don't run out of stored hot water

A traditional water heater stores a fixed amount of hot water.

If you have a 50-gallon tank and your household uses that hot water faster than the heater can recover, eventually the shower gets cold.

A tankless water heater works differently. It heats water as it passes through the unit.

That is where the term "endless hot water" comes from.

As long as the unit is properly sized and can keep up with the amount of hot water being used, it can continue producing hot water without waiting for a tank to refill and recover.

For households that regularly run out of hot water, that can be a major improvement.

## But "endless" does not mean unlimited

Every tankless water heater still has a maximum flow rate.

If multiple showers, faucets and appliances are all using hot water at the same time, the unit has to be large enough to handle that demand.

That is why tankless sizing matters.

We look at things such as the number of bathrooms, how many fixtures may run at the same time, and the temperature rise the heater needs to produce.

A properly sized tankless system can provide excellent performance.

An undersized system can be frustrating.

## Tankless takes up much less space

This is one of the most obvious benefits.

Instead of having a 40-, 50- or 75-gallon tank taking up floor space, a tankless unit mounts on the wall.

For some homeowners, recovering that garage or utility-room space is a major reason to make the switch.

## Tankless usually costs more upfront

This is one of the biggest reasons we often recommend staying with a tank.

Replacing one conventional gas tank with another is usually a relatively straightforward job.

Converting to tankless is different.

Depending on the house, the project may require:

- Gas-line modifications
- New venting
- Condensate drainage
- Electrical work
- Relocation or reconfiguration of nearby plumbing

That is why we generally inspect a home before providing a firm tankless-conversion estimate.

As a broad Bay Area range, a gas tankless installation is often around **$3,500 to $6,000+**, depending on the amount of conversion work required.

A standard gas tank replacement is generally around **$1,700 to $3,000+**.

If your existing tank setup works well and your main goal is simply to restore reliable hot water, another tank is usually the less expensive path.

## The gas line can be a major part of the conversion

A conventional residential gas tank water heater typically uses much less gas than a high-output tankless unit.

Many tankless models can approach 200,000 BTU per hour.

That does not automatically mean the entire gas line has to be replaced, but the gas system has to be evaluated properly.

Pipe size, distance from the meter, gas pressure, and other gas appliances in the home all matter.

This is one reason tankless conversion pricing can vary significantly from one house to another.

## Tankless also changes the venting

Tankless water heaters do not simply connect to every existing tank-water-heater vent.

Depending on the model and location, the system may require completely new venting.

Condensing tankless units also produce condensate that has to be drained correctly.

Sometimes these changes are simple.

Sometimes they make up a significant portion of the conversion cost.

## Does tankless save money on energy?

Tankless systems are generally more efficient than conventional gas storage water heaters because they do not continuously keep a large tank of water hot.

But we would not recommend spending thousands of dollars on a tankless conversion based on energy savings alone.

The actual savings depend on hot-water usage, utility rates, the equipment being compared, and how long you stay in the home.

For many homeowners, the stronger reasons to choose tankless are continuous hot water, space savings, and potential equipment longevity.

Energy efficiency is a benefit, but it should not be the only reason for making the switch.

## Tankless water heaters need maintenance too

Tankless does not mean maintenance-free.

Minerals can build up inside the heat exchanger over time, especially in areas with harder water.

Manufacturers generally recommend periodic flushing or descaling, with the exact interval depending on water quality and usage.

That maintenance should be factored into the long-term ownership cost.

## Tankless systems can last a long time

A quality tankless water heater can potentially last significantly longer than a conventional tank.

Manufacturers often cite useful service lives approaching 20 years under good conditions.

Traditional tank water heaters more commonly fall into the 8- to 12-year range, although some last considerably longer.

That sounds like a strong argument for tankless, but in the Bay Area there is now another factor that matters just as much.

## The 2031 Bay Area rule changes the tankless calculation

Under the Bay Area Air Quality Management District's current Rule 9-6 schedule, water heaters between 75,000 and 2 million BTU per hour, which includes most residential gas tankless water heaters, are scheduled to become subject to zero-NOx requirements beginning January 1, 2031.

That matters a lot if you are thinking about converting today.

A tankless conversion can involve thousands of dollars in additional work for gas supply, venting, condensate drainage, and installation.

You may be making that investment for a gas system that, under the current rule, may not be replaceable with another conventional gas tankless unit once the next equipment cycle comes around.

In other words, you may only get one cycle out of that conversion.

That does not mean installing a gas tankless water heater today is automatically a bad decision.

If you really value continuous hot water, need the space, or have another strong reason to go tankless, it can still make sense.

But it is one of the main reasons we generally do not push Bay Area homeowners toward tankless conversions right now.

## Why we often recommend staying with a tank

If your existing gas tank has been meeting your needs, replacing it with another tank is often the simplest and most economical option.

You avoid the cost of a tankless conversion.

You avoid paying for gas-line and venting changes that may only benefit one equipment cycle.

And you keep your options open while Bay Area water-heating regulations continue to evolve.

That can be especially important for homeowners who may eventually need to move toward heat-pump water heating anyway.

## When tankless still makes sense

There are still homeowners for whom tankless is a very good fit.

It can make sense if:

- You regularly run out of hot water
- You have a large household with sustained hot-water demand
- Space is extremely valuable
- You are already remodeling
- You strongly prefer continuous hot water
- The conversion is relatively simple
- You understand the Bay Area regulatory timeline and still prefer tankless

In those situations, tankless can be a very good system.

We just think homeowners should make that decision with the full picture in mind.

## What about a large 75-gallon tank?

Sometimes homeowners with large families assume tankless is their only option.

Not necessarily.

A 75-gallon tank can deliver a substantial amount of hot water and may be much easier to install if the house is already designed for one.

For some families, a large tank gives them the capacity they need without the added cost and complexity of converting to tankless.

We look at how the household actually uses hot water rather than assuming one technology is always better.

## Should you convert to tankless?

In the current Bay Area regulatory environment, our default recommendation is usually to stay with a tank if your existing tank setup works well.

That is especially true if you would be spending a significant amount of money converting solely because tankless sounds like an upgrade.

Tankless is not automatically better.

It is a different system with different advantages, costs, and long-term considerations.

If you are still interested in making the switch, we will look at your home, explain exactly what the conversion would involve, and tell you whether we think it makes sense.

Sometimes it does.

Sometimes another tank is the smarter choice.

## Replace existing: “Why Choosing a Professional Water Heater Over Big Box Stores Saves You Money”

# Should You Buy a Water Heater From a Big-Box Store or From Your Plumber?

We get this question fairly often:

**“Wouldn’t it be cheaper if I buy the water heater myself from Home Depot or Lowe’s and just pay you to install it?”**

Sometimes it can save money upfront.

But there are two things homeowners should understand before doing it: the equipment may not be built to the same specification as the models plumbers normally buy through professional supply houses, and if something goes wrong with the heater itself, you become responsible for dealing with the retailer or manufacturer.

That can make a seemingly simple way to save money much more complicated.

## Big-box and professional-distribution heaters are not always the same

Water heaters can look very similar from the outside while using different components.

One easy example is the drain valve.

A number of A. O. Smith Signature heaters sold through Lowe’s use nylon drain valves. Some Rheem models sold through Home Depot have also used plastic drain valves.

By comparison, the Bradford White heaters we normally install use a brass drain valve.

That may sound like a small detail, but it is a good example of how manufacturers can build products to different specifications and price points.

We prefer equipment purchased through professional plumbing suppliers because we know the product line, the parts, and the supply chain behind it.

## The bigger issue is what happens if something fails

We recently installed an A. O. Smith water heater that a customer had purchased from Lowe’s.

About three months later, something in the gas-control system failed.

The problem was not with our installation. It was an equipment issue.

Because the homeowner had purchased the heater herself, she had to work through Lowe’s warranty process to resolve it.

That became especially problematic because this was a rental property with a tenant living in the home. A warranty process that stretches over several days can mean several days without hot water while the owner coordinates between the retailer, manufacturer, installer, and tenant.

That is exactly the situation we try to avoid.

If Water Heater Boys supplies the heater and there is a legitimate equipment failure, we can work directly through our supplier and warranty channels and generally move much faster toward getting the hot water restored.

## Customer-supplied equipment changes who is responsible

When we install a water heater that we supply, we are standing behind both the equipment we selected and the installation we performed.

With a customer-supplied heater, our responsibility is the installation.

If there is a water leak or gas leak caused by our work, that is obviously our responsibility.

But if the heater later develops an internal problem, such as an ignition issue, gas-control failure, pilot problem, or another equipment defect, additional Water Heater Boys visits to diagnose or address that problem are billable.

The homeowner is also responsible for handling the equipment warranty with the retailer or manufacturer.

That distinction is important when deciding whether the upfront savings are worth it.

## Buying your own heater also means choosing the right model

Water heaters that look almost identical online can have important differences in height, diameter, BTU input, recovery rate, venting, and installation requirements.

When we supply the heater, we select the model based on the home and the existing installation.

If a homeowner purchases the wrong unit, there may be a return, exchange, delay, or additional work required to make it fit.

## What does Water Heater Boys include?

For a standard tank replacement, our pricing includes the water heater, new water supply lines, a new gas line and shutoff valve, a new drain pan, a sediment trap, removal of the old heater, and tax.

Permit costs or additional work such as vent repairs, expansion tanks, recirculation work, or difficult access may be separate depending on the job.

So when comparing prices, don't compare only the retail price of the tank.

Compare the complete installed price, the equipment being installed, and what happens if that equipment develops a problem afterward.

## Can we install a water heater you already purchased?

Yes, in some situations.

But if you have not bought the heater yet, we generally recommend letting us supply it.

You may spend a little more than buying a promotional unit from a big-box store yourself, but you are also getting equipment we are familiar with, professional supplier support, and a much cleaner warranty process if something goes wrong.

For most homeowners, especially landlords or anyone who cannot afford to have a house without hot water for several days, that has real value.

If you are comparing a store-bought water heater with a Water Heater Boys quote, send us the model number. We can tell you whether it is appropriate for your home and explain exactly what is included in our price.

## Replace existing: “Understanding Tankless Heater Pricing”

# How Much Does a Tankless Water Heater Cost in the Bay Area?

One of the harder water heater jobs to price over the phone is a tank-to-tankless conversion.

The tankless unit itself is only part of the cost. What really determines the final price is how much work your house needs to support it.

In the Bay Area, a typical gas tankless installation is often in the **$3,500 to $6,000+** range, but some conversions can go higher.

Here is what usually makes the difference.

## Replacing tankless with tankless is very different from converting a tank

If you already have a tankless water heater and are replacing it with a similar unit in the same location, much of the necessary infrastructure may already be there.

That can make the job relatively straightforward.

Converting from a traditional gas tank to tankless is different.

Now we have to look at the gas supply, venting, condensate drainage, water lines, electrical requirements, and installation location.

That is why we generally do an on-site visit before providing a final quote for a tankless conversion.

## Gas supply can be one of the biggest variables

A standard gas tank water heater may have an input around 40,000 BTU per hour.

A high-capacity tankless unit can approach 200,000 BTU per hour.

That is a major difference.

Sometimes an existing gas line can support the new tankless unit. Sometimes it cannot.

We have to look at the pipe size, length of the run, gas pressure, and what other appliances share the system.

If a new or larger gas line has to be run from the meter, that can add significantly to the cost.

## Venting can be simple or complicated

Tankless water heaters have different venting requirements from traditional atmospheric gas tanks.

Depending on the model and location, we may need to install new intake and exhaust venting through an exterior wall or roof.

How difficult that is depends heavily on where the water heater is located.

A garage installation with easy access to an exterior wall can be relatively simple.

A heater buried deep inside the house can be a very different project.

## Condensing tankless units need drainage

Most high-efficiency gas tankless units produce condensate during operation.

That condensate needs somewhere to go.

Sometimes there is an easy nearby drain.

Other times we need to run a new drain line or use a condensate pump.

Depending on the equipment and drainage system, a condensate neutralizer may also be appropriate or required.

This is another reason we want to see the installation before giving a firm conversion price.

## Location and access matter

A tankless unit mounted on an accessible garage wall is generally easier to install than one in an attic, crawlspace, tight closet, or difficult interior location.

Long plumbing runs, difficult vent routes, and limited access all add labor.

Sometimes relocating the tankless unit actually makes the installation cleaner, but moving it also means extending water, gas, electrical, and drainage connections.

## The size of the tankless unit matters, but not as much as the installation

Larger tankless units generally cost more than smaller ones, but the difference in equipment price is often not the biggest factor in the overall project.

The expensive part can be modifying the house to support the system.

That is why two homes installing the exact same tankless water heater can end up with very different total prices.

## Why can two contractors give very different quotes?

Sometimes the scope really is different.

One contractor may be planning a new gas line while another believes the existing line can be used.

One quote may include new venting, condensate drainage, a neutralizer, permits, or other work that another quote handles differently.

So when you compare tankless estimates, ask what is actually included.

But there is another reality: plumbing companies also have different overhead and pricing structures.

A higher price does not automatically mean a better installation, just as a lower price does not automatically mean corners are being cut.

The best comparison is the actual equipment and complete scope of work.

## One more thing to consider before spending money on a conversion

If you are in the Bay Area, there is also a long-term regulatory issue.

Under the current Bay Area Air Quality Management District schedule, most residential gas tankless water heaters are expected to become subject to zero-NOx requirements beginning in 2031.

That means if you spend several thousand dollars converting a traditional tank setup to gas tankless today, you may only get one equipment cycle out of that conversion before the available replacement options change.

For that reason, Water Heater Boys generally does not push customers toward tankless unless there is a specific reason they really want it, such as continuous hot water or recovering valuable space.

If your existing gas tank setup works well for your household, another tank may be the better financial decision.

## How do we price a tankless conversion?

For tankless conversions, we prefer to see the property.

We look at:

- The existing gas supply
- The proposed tankless location
- Venting options
- Condensate drainage
- Water-line routing
- Electrical availability
- Access to the work area
- The household's hot-water demand

Once we see those things, we can give you a much more meaningful price than a generic number over the phone.

If you are considering a tankless conversion, contact Water Heater Boys and we can take a look at the installation and explain what the project would actually require.

## Replace existing: “Is It Time to Replace Your Toilet?”

# When Should You Replace a Toilet Instead of Repairing It?

Most toilet problems can be repaired.

A running toilet may only need a flapper or fill valve. A loose handle is usually simple. So we do not recommend replacing a toilet just because it is old.

But there are times when replacement makes more sense.

# Is the Bay Area Banning Gas Water Heaters? What Homeowners Need to Know

There has been a lot of confusing information about whether gas water heaters are being "banned" in the Bay Area.

In 2023, the Bay Area Air Quality Management District adopted new zero-NOx standards for water heaters.

Under the rule currently on the books:

- Smaller water heaters under 75,000 BTU per hour, which includes most standard residential gas tank water heaters, are scheduled to become subject to the zero-NOx standard beginning January 1, 2027.
- Larger water heaters from 75,000 BTU up to 2 million BTU per hour, including most gas tankless water heaters, are scheduled to become subject to the standard beginning January 1, 2031.

This is where a lot of the current confusion comes from.

The proposal also includes exemptions for some situations where electrical capacity, space constraints, or financial hardship would make conversion particularly difficult.

As of now, those amendments have **not yet been adopted**.

The Air District's Board is tentatively scheduled to consider them in November 2026.

So for now:

**2027 is still the adopted date.  
2028 is the proposed new date.**

We would not tell a homeowner that the rule has officially been delayed until the Board actually adopts the amendment.

## Does this mean you have to switch to a heat pump water heater?

Not today.

If your existing gas water heater is working, you can keep using it.

If it fails before the applicable rule takes effect, a conventional gas replacement may still be available.

After the zero-NOx requirement applies, the available replacement options will depend on the rules in effect at that time, any exemptions, and what compliant equipment is available on the market.

Today, heat pump water heaters are the main residential zero-NOx option.

That does not necessarily mean every Bay Area home will be forced into the exact same solution.

The proposed amendments are specifically trying to address homes where electrification is unusually difficult or expensive.

## What about gas tankless water heaters?

Tankless has a different timeline.

Most residential gas tankless systems are rated above 75,000 BTU per hour, which puts them in the category currently scheduled for zero-NOx requirements beginning January 1, 2031.

Usually, we would not replace a perfectly good water heater solely because a regulation is coming.

If your gas tank is already getting old, it is worth understanding your options before it fails.

A heat pump conversion can require electrical work, condensate drainage, and enough physical space and airflow for the new heater.

Those are easier decisions to make when you are not standing in a garage with a leaking tank and no hot water.

## What happens if my water heater fails after the rule changes?

That will depend on the final version of the rule and your particular home.

Some houses will be relatively easy to convert to a heat pump water heater.

Others may need substantial electrical work or have physical limitations that make the conversion more difficult.

The Air District's proposed 2026 amendments are intended to create more flexibility for some of those situations.

That is why we think homeowners should be cautious about headlines that say simply, "Gas water heaters are banned."

The actual rule is more complicated than that.

For most homeowners, there is no reason to panic or replace a working water heater.

The practical approach is:

Know how old your current heater is.

## What about the new Bay Area water heater rules?

There has been a lot of confusion about the Bay Area's upcoming rules for gas water heaters.

The Bay Area Air Quality Management District adopted zero-NOx requirements that currently have an implementation date beginning January 1, 2027 for small water heaters under 75,000 BTU per hour, which generally includes conventional residential tank water heaters.

However, the Air District has proposed amendments that would move that date to January 2028 and create additional exemptions for certain situations. The Air District's Board is tentatively scheduled to consider those amendments in November 2026.

So, as of now, the 2028 date is proposed but not yet final.

The rule also does not mean homeowners have to remove a working gas water heater. It applies when covered equipment is newly installed or replaced.

Because these requirements are still evolving, we check the current rules before recommending replacement options.

## The cheapest water heater is not always the cheapest job

It is easy to focus on the cost of the water heater itself, but the equipment is only one part of the project.

That becomes especially important when comparing a standard gas tank, tankless system and hybrid heat pump water heater.

The right choice depends on the existing home, installation cost, hot-water demand, available space, utility costs and how long you expect to stay in the property.

There is no single water heater that is the best choice for every Bay Area home.
