# What Bay Area Property Managers Should Know About the New Water Heater Rules

For property managers, the upcoming Bay Area water heater rules are less about one individual replacement and more about planning.

If you manage multiple homes or units, the key question is not whether every gas water heater needs to be replaced right now.

They do not.

The real issue is knowing which properties may become difficult or expensive to convert when a water heater eventually fails.

## What is changing?

The Bay Area Air Quality Management District adopted zero-NOx standards for new water heaters.

Under the rule currently in effect:

- Water heaters under 75,000 BTU per hour, which includes most standard residential gas tanks, are scheduled to become subject to the zero-NOx standard beginning January 1, 2027.
- Water heaters between 75,000 and 2 million BTU per hour, including most gas tankless systems, are scheduled for January 1, 2031.

The Air District has proposed delaying the smaller-water-heater date from January 2027 to January 2028 and adding exemptions for some situations where electrical capacity, space, or cost make conversion especially difficult. That proposal has not yet been formally adopted.

Existing working gas water heaters do not have to be removed simply because of the rule.

## Why property managers should care now

A normal tank-for-tank replacement can often be handled quickly.

A gas-to-heat-pump conversion may require more planning.

Depending on the property, the job can involve:

- A new 240-volt electrical circuit
- Electrical panel capacity
- Condensate drainage
- Adequate space and airflow
- Coordination with the owner or tenant
- Possible electrical or building modifications

Those are not things you want to discover for the first time after a tenant calls to say the water heater is leaking.

## Identify the difficult properties before they become emergencies

If you manage several properties, it makes sense to know which ones are easy candidates for heat-pump water heaters and which ones may be more complicated.

A garage installation with a nearby electrical panel may be relatively straightforward.

A water heater in a tight interior closet, an older building with limited electrical capacity, or a property with a long circuit run may require much more work.

That does not mean the heater needs to be replaced early.

It just means you know what you are dealing with before there is an emergency.

## Tenant disruption matters

A failed water heater is already disruptive.

A conversion that unexpectedly requires an electrician, panel work, or multiple visits can make that disruption much worse.

For rental properties, speed matters because the tenant needs hot water restored as quickly as possible.

That is one reason advance planning is valuable.

If we already understand the property and know what the future replacement is likely to require, the decision can be made much faster when the heater finally fails.

## Budgeting becomes easier too

Water heater replacement costs can vary substantially depending on whether the job is a straightforward tank replacement or a conversion.

For general Bay Area planning, we typically see:

| Type | General installed range |
| --- | ---: |
| Standard gas tank water heater | $1,700–$3,000+ |
| Hybrid heat pump water heater | $4,000–$7,000+ |
| Gas tankless water heater | $3,500–$6,000+ |

Electrical upgrades, difficult access, relocation, drainage work, or other property-specific conditions can increase the price.

For owners with multiple properties, knowing ahead of time which units may require additional work makes capital planning much easier.

## What about gas tankless?

Most residential gas tankless water heaters fall into the category currently scheduled for the zero-NOx requirement beginning in 2031.

That is worth considering before spending heavily to convert a rental property from a standard gas tank to gas tankless today.

You may be investing in gas-line, venting, and condensate work for a system that could face different replacement options when that unit eventually reaches the end of its life.

For that reason, we generally do not recommend tankless conversions for rental properties unless there is a specific operational reason to choose tankless.

## You do not need to replace good heaters early

We are not recommending that property managers start replacing functioning gas water heaters simply because the rules are changing.

In most cases, the better strategy is:

Know the age of the heater.

Know whether the property is likely to be easy or difficult to electrify.

Have a rough replacement plan.

Then replace the heater when it actually needs to be replaced.
